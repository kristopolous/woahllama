32K open ollamas, 256 honeypots, and 365 failed ransoms

Eighteen months of unsecured Ollama servers, reconstructed from the commit history of three scanners and then probed directly. Hundreds of them are not servers at all. They are one honeypot kit wearing an Ollama mask, built to catch the people scanning for open models. Another 365 carry a ransom demand for files that were never encrypted. The wallet has never received a satoshi.

Last updated: September 10, 2026

Downloaded a lot, or left running a lot

The gap is the chart. A model far to the right of its download share is one people leave running; far to the left, one they pull and move on from.

A pull count only ever goes up, so over the whole library the oldest models win by having been there longest and nothing recent can rank at all. Published in the last year renormalises both sides over a recent cohort, which is where adoption is actually visible.

How many were actually out there

A single scanner only ever catches a slice of the live population on any given day, so the raw count understates it and jumps around with coverage. Feeding every host's sightings and its later liveness check into the survival model gives a smoother estimate of how many servers were probably alive each month. Chapter 2

Unexpected behavior

Scan for long enough and you start to distrust your own results. We went looking for people running Ollama on the open internet, and a large fraction of what came back does not behave like anybody running anything.

Nobody reported this. It turned up as arithmetic that would not sit still. So: here are the counts, here are the two separate things the counts turn out to be, and then each one in turn. Both of them come apart in the end, though the bigger one held out for eighteen months and only gave way when we stopped asking it about Ollama.

Something is wrong with a lot of these

Start with the size of it, before anybody tries to explain anything. Take every server that reported a model list and run four checks a normal install passes without thinking. Is the catalogue a fixed list turning up word for word on thousands of machines that have never met? Are the models named like scratch files? Are they commercial products whose weights were never released to anyone? Does the file size the server reports match the file that actually exists?

That is a lot of servers failing a very low bar. None of it is an accusation about any particular machine, and some of it is surely somebody testing something on a Tuesday. Every chart in Chapter 1 lets you throw these out and watch what happens. What is left over is the question of what they are, which is the rest of this chapter.

Two kinds of anomaly

The first useful thing we found is that this is not one phenomenon. Sort the flagged machines by what they actually advertise and they fall into two piles with almost nothing to do with each other.

Pile one: old models, on servers that are not servers. A frozen pool of unremarkable models from 2023 and 2024, the same short list everywhere: llama2:latest, llama3:latest, openchat:7b, codellama:13b, qwen2.5:1.5b. Sizes constant to the byte across machines with no connection to each other. Version strings drawn from a small rotating set. Ask one to hold a conversation and whatever answers is not a language model. This is the bulk of it, and it comes and goes in waves.

Pile two: commercial models that cannot exist. Servers offering gpt-4o, claude-3-opus and gpt-4. Neither OpenAI nor Anthropic has ever released those weights, to anyone, so whatever sits under those names is something else wearing the label. Far fewer of these, and here is the odd part: they are on ordinary working servers, serving real models quite happily alongside.

Different models, different machines, different behaviour, different scale. The only thing the two piles share is that both are wrong. We take them in that order.

Chapter 2.1

The servers that are not servers

This is the big pile. Hundreds of hosts answering the Ollama API from what is very obviously a script: one frozen model list, identical file sizes, version strings off a wheel, and chat replies stitched together out of a phrase bank. Everything below is what they do.

It took a long time to work out why, and the answer arrived from a direction we were not looking in. The fake Ollama is not the point of these machines. It is one lure on a trap built for the people scanning for open Ollama servers, which is to say for us.

Hundreds of hosts answering as Ollama

OllamaSpider scrapes Shodan and grabs endpoints on ports that were never Ollama. The other two feeds verify their hosts and do not carry this. Open Ollama servers are not mostly fake, and there are thousands of real ones. This is a claim about a specific set of hosts in a specific feed.

These hosts are live and responding. Something on each one answers the Ollama API with a model list, a version string, a chat reply. All of them advertise some subset of one small, fixed pool of old, unremarkable models (llama2:latest, llama3:latest, openchat:7b, qwen2.5:1.5b and a couple of others), frozen since early 2024, each at a size constant to the byte wherever it appears.

Ask one a question and the reply reads like filler from a small phrase bank rather than a model's answer. Ask its version and you get one of four fixed strings. Ask a model for its details and the response is empty, yet the running-model endpoint reports every one of them loaded and running at once, which no real host does.

Ask it to run a model that does not exist and it answers anyway; tell it to pull or delete a model and it reports success while nothing changes. Every command fails the same way: it always says yes. It is hundreds of hosts actively answering, and answering the same narrow way.

Asking for the version

The sharpest test does not come from the scanners. In a fresh snapshot of 4,407 hosts that answer as Ollama, ollama version splits the population in two. Real servers report one of 172 different versions in a natural long tail. The responders return one of just four fixed strings, which stand as a tight near-equal band far above every real version. The four are in orange.

So what is it?

    
Every test above asks the same kind of question. We spoke Ollama at
    these machines, in more and more careful ways, and got nonsense back. The thing we
    never did was speak anything else at them.

    
A real Ollama daemon serves its own short list of /api/
    routes and returns 404 for everything else. So ask one of these hosts for the four
    things a scanner always asks for instead. A WordPress login page. A leaked
    .git config. A .env file. A database dump left in the web
    root. None of them has anything to do with Ollama, and a real Ollama host has none of
    them.

    
They serve all four.

    

    

    

  


  

    
It is a trap, and the fake Ollama is one wall of it

    
This is not a broken Ollama. It is a honeypot that answers every
    protocol you try, and Ollama is one of the personalities it wears. That reframes every
    finding above. A catalogue frozen since 2024 is not neglect, it is a prop. Sizes that
    do not match the published blobs are not a bug in somebody's mock, they are numbers
    nobody expected to be checked against ollama.com. The phrase bank is not a failed
    language model, it is enough of one to keep a scanner reading for another few seconds
    while it is logged. Every command succeeds because the point is to never give you a
    reason to leave.

    
The pages give up the operator. Compare the bodies byte for byte
    across the machines and two of the four are the same file on nearly all of them, which
    makes this one kit deployed repeatedly rather than many people doing the same thing.
    The other two are different on every single one, and those are exactly the two that
    carry credentials.

    

    

  


  

    
The bait has been restocked

    
One last thing the sweep turned up, which is the strongest evidence
    that somebody is still tending this. The live fleet now splits almost exactly in half
    by what it advertises.

    

    

  


  

    
What we still cannot say

    
Who runs it. The kit matches no published honeypot, tarpit or
    mock-server project, checked against GitHub, the web, and the honeypot literature. The
    closest, funnypot, echoes the model name rather than the prompt and runs
    on a single host. Whether this is one security vendor's canary network, one researcher
    at scale, or something with a less friendly purpose, the traffic alone does not say.

    
Nor whether it works. The whole design assumes somebody scrapes an
    exposed host, finds a credential, and tries it. We are not going to find out by trying
    one.

  






  
It arrives in waves

  
A fair objection to all of this is that we simply got better at scanning
  and the fleet was always there. So count it as a share of everything visible on the same
  day instead. Our own coverage cancels out, and if the shape survives that, the shape is
  real.

  
It survives. Nothing at all before April 2025, a peak that summer, an
  almost total disappearance over the winter, then a second climb through 2026 that is now
  bigger than the first.

  
Which kills the most boring explanation available. This is not one broken
  config copied around in 2025 and left to rot, because a thing left to rot does not stop
  and start again. Something turned it off. Something turned it back on.

  
That reads differently now. A trap is deployed, run for a season, torn
  down, and deployed again with the bait refreshed. The waves are campaigns.

  

    

      
      
    

    

    

  





  
Where they are hosted

  
Here is the one thing about this fleet we can say without hedging. Every
  phantom-catalogue host that our FOFA capture also saw is on AWS. Not most of them. All of
  them. The same capture holds ten thousand Ollama servers hosted somewhere that is not AWS,
  and the number of those carrying the phantom catalogue is zero.

  
Spread proportionally, a couple of thousand should be sitting on Hetzner
  and OVH and the rest. Go and look. They are not there.

  
One account, one cloud, one kit, one set of tokens. The hosting is not
  a demographic fact about who runs fake Ollama servers. It is the operator's invoice.

  

    

    

    

  






  
Chapter 2.2

  
The commercial models that cannot exist

  
The small pile. Like the big one, it gave way to a single HTTP
  request we should have sent much earlier, and to a different endpoint than the one we
  kept interrogating.

  
Below: what these servers claim, the two sensible explanations for
  it, why the evidence refuses to support either, and then what is actually in there.





  
Servers wearing models they cannot run


  
Probing live hosts directly turned up a separate oddity. Hundreds of
  real, working Ollama boxes advertise a closed-weights model that cannot exist on
  Ollama: gpt-4, gpt-4o, claude-3-opus, and newer
  names like claude-opus-4.8, gpt-5.5 and
  gemini-3.5-flash. Of the live hosts we reached, 726 carried at least one
  of these.

  
They never appear alone. Every one of those 726 also runs genuine open
  models, usually Qwen, DeepSeek, or an abliterated Huihui build, so these are not empty
  decoys but real servers with fake names planted among the real ones. And the fake names
  carry no hf.co/ prefix the way the genuine models on the same boxes do, so
  they are made on the machine with ollama cp or a Modelfile, not pulled from
  Hugging Face. There is no upstream repo claiming to be GPT-4. It is local theatre.

  
Two very different levels of effort sit behind it. On 708 hosts the
  premium name points at a real but tiny 638 MB, 1.1B, Q4_0 llama blob, a small
  model wearing a big label. About 500 of them share one byte-identical blob, so this is a
  single artifact copied across hundreds of machines, not hundreds of separate pranks. A
  smaller, more deliberate seven fabricate the manifest outright: zero-byte entries whose
  parameter_size is the literal string synthetic, listing
  current premium models they demonstrably do not hold, each sitting beside per-host
  gateway-diagnostic entries. That tier looks like a catalogue kept
  up to date on purpose, or a scanner planting its own write-test marker. We cannot tell
  which from here.

  
One detail gives the game away: which names get faked. Whenever a real
  model actually sits behind the fake label, the label is a 2024 trophy, almost always
  gpt-4, gpt-4o or claude-3-opus. The current
  flagships, claude-opus-4.8 or gpt-5.5, turn up only as the
  empty synthetic manifests, never with weights behind them. So nobody parks a cheap model
  behind a current name and invites you to test it. The impersonation is frozen where it
  started, reaching for models that were the state of the art when these boxes were set up
  and have since been passed by weights anyone can download for free. Even the successful
  version of this lie now points at something a small open model would beat.

  
The fake names are not the only small thing on these boxes. Setting the
  fakes aside and measuring the real models, hosts carrying a premium name run
  noticeably smaller models than clean hosts: a median of 1.9 GB against 4.7 GB,
  with 42% under a gigabyte versus 16%. They also cluster on old Ollama versions, a fifth
  of them on 0.30.2 alone, where clean hosts spread across many including current
  releases. So the premium label tends to sit on an older, lighter, less maintained box.

The explanations that do not survive

  
There is a completely mundane explanation available and we went looking
  for it first. Plenty of closed-source software ships a hardcoded list of OpenAI model
  names with no way to change it. Point it at your own machine with
  OPENAI_BASE_URL, run ollama cp to give one of your models the
  name the software insists on, and it works. This is not a hack somebody clever invented.
  Ollama documents it.

  
Good theory. It makes predictions. Every one of them is wrong.

  

    

  

  
So the tidy explanations leave no mark anywhere we would expect to find
  one. That is not the same as knowing what this is. It only rules things out, which is
  most of what this chapter can offer.





  
What the commercial models actually are

  
So we stopped theorising and asked one. Ollama has an endpoint,
  /api/show, that hands back exactly what a model is: what it was copied from,
  the architecture inside the file, and any system prompt somebody attached to it. It is one
  curl. In hindsight this should have been the first thing we tried rather than
  the last.

  
Every single one of them is tinyllama. A 638 MB model
  from 2023 that runs comfortably on a laptop, wearing a GPT-4 badge, with about
  1.25 kB of text stapled to the front.

  
The text is a ransom note. It announces that the machine has been
  compromised and asks for Bitcoin. It is installed as the system prompt and as a
  pre-seeded conversation, so the model has already agreed, in writing, to repeat the demand
  in every answer it ever gives. Nothing is encrypted. Nothing is deleted. Several of these
  machines are cheerfully serving real 27-billion-parameter models the whole time.

What it earned

    

  

  
If you got here by searching that wallet address, this part is for
  you. We published it on purpose. If you found this note on a machine you run,
  searching the address is exactly what you would do next, and you deserve to land somewhere
  that tells you the truth. It has never been paid, by anybody. Nothing on your server was
  encrypted or deleted. The demand is a bluff and you can ignore it.

  
What does deserve your attention is how it got there. Your Ollama API was
  reachable from the open internet and accepted writes without asking anyone for a password,
  which means anybody at all could install a model on your machine. This particular person
  left a note asking for money. The next one might not. Close the port, and delete the
  models you did not put there.
